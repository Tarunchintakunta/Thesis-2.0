                                                       Abstract
              Operating cost of the cloud is now a significant factor and Amazon S3 has reactive features:
          Lifecycle Policies are based on age, and Intelligent-Tiering is only triggered when inactivity is de-
          tected, but neither feature makes an assumption about what it will cost you to keep the data on the
          cloud, nor does it estimate savings before the feature kicks in. We propose a Predictive Storage Cost
          Optimization Framework that combines the three components of the storage-class recommendation,
          time-series cost forecasting, and savings estimation into a single AWS-native pipeline, run across
          real S3 data instead of simulated data. It is novel in combining three functions that AWS currently
          provides as separate and unreconciled services (Lifecycle Policies, Intelligent-Tiering and Cost Ex-
          plorer) under one roof and auditable tool to make decisions, while also keeping a naive forecasting
          baseline to prevent claims of accuracy that are too good to be true. The framework will be tested
          with accuracy of storage class allocation, forecast error, and the savings achieved using it as com-
          pared to AWS-native storage tiers, to give empirical evidence of the value of proactive vs reactive
          management of S3 storage for cloud FinOps practice.
          Keywords: Cloud Storage Optimization, Amazon S3, Predictive Cost Forecasting, Intelligent Tier-
          ing, FinOps, AWS, combined framework.


1       Introduction
As this adoption continues to grow, storage cost is one of the biggest, recurring components of cloud
operating budgets, as organisations increasingly turn to Amazon S3 to store their growing data volumes,
particularly those that are large (Liu et al., 2025). The S3 family of storage classes range from Standard
(fastest retrieval time) to Glacier Deep Archive (slowest retrieval time), allowing for a balance of storage
costs and retrieval speed. The number of objects and the access patterns change, and it’s hard to deter-
mine if the proper class is selected for each object manually. Both of AWS’s native solutions (Lifecycle
Policies and Intelligent-Tiering) are reactive: Lifecycle Policies move objects to a colder tier after a
certain point, even if that point is not based on actual future access1 , and Intelligent-Tiering moves an
object to a colder tier after a period of inactivity has been detected2 . Both are non-predictive: neither
predicts what an object’s storage cost will be next month, nor estimates the amount of objects that will
be saved if a given optimisation action is taken. This reactive vs proactive cost management gap is the
area of focus for this research.
Storage cost management is important, as FinOps - the practice of balancing cloud costs and busi-

Research Question
To what extent does an integrated predictive storage-class optimisation framework – combining machine-
learning-based storage-class recommendation, time-series-based cost forecasting, and savings estima-
tion – reduce Amazon S3 storage cost and improve storage-class allocation accuracy, relative to AWS-
native Lifecycle Policies and Intelligent-Tiering, when evaluated in a live AWS environment?

Proposed Solution
The research suggests a five-step framework, implemented entirely on AWS: (1) a metadata collection
module on top of the AWS SDK (Boto3) that collects attributes of individual objects; (2) a hybrid rule-
based and machine-learning based recommendation engine that proposes the most cost-efficient storage
class for every object; (3) a time-series forecasting module that estimates the future storage cost; (4)
a savings-estimation component that quantifies the expected savings of each recommendation before
applying it; and (5) an evaluation harness that compares the decisions of the proposed framework with
those of Lifecycle Policies and Intelligent-Tiering on the same bucket contents.

Contribution
Today, Lifecycle Policies, Intelligent-Tiering and Cost Explorer are three separate AWS services, and
an administrator has to reconcile them manually referencing tiering decisions to a completely differ-
ent console for cost reporting that does not have a shared data model between the two. This research
brings together storage-class recommendation, forward cost forecasting and savings estimation into a
single decision-support pipeline, rather than having three signals, one for each, to interpret by hand, and
validates the research end-to-end on the live infrastructure of S3 instead of through simulation.
More specifically, there are three contributions. First and foremost, a technical contribution; a re-usable,
meta-annotation-based recommendation engine that, in addition to the deployment-strategy evaluation
that is natively supported, also enables a supervised classifier that can be deployed alongside it, and
thus provide a more active recommendation capability than a mere comparative benchmark. Second, a
methodological contribution: a protocol to evaluate forecast accuracy as compared to the naive persis-
tence benchmark that has seldom been considered in applied cloud-cost studies and which is identified
by Beck, Dovern and Vogl (2025) as a risk of inflated forecast accuracy. Third, a practical contribution:
empirical, quantified evidence simulated projections as well as realised cost savings, allocation accu-
racy – that shows the benefits of proactive, forecast-driven storage-tier management over the reactive
native tooling AWS provides, with clear relevance to FinOps practitioners who are evaluating whether
spending time on such tooling is worthwhile.
This work is the first to combine and test all three functions jointly for Amazon S3 empirically, while
each of the previous works optimise placement, cost and/or predictive FinOps in isolation.

2     Literature Review
2.1   Reactive AWS-Native Storage Optimisation
AWS has two key reactive storage optimisation features: at the extremes of the spectrum. Lifecycle
Policies are calendar-based rules applied to any object, are deterministic and do not depend on the use.
With Intelligent-Tiering, you don’t need to manually designate access tiers for objects, because it mon-
itors access activity and automatically moves objects between tiers based on the time they haven’t been
accessed. The drawbacks of both Lifecycle Policies and Intelligent-Tiering are an inability to know what
is actually being used, and an inability to act proactively once a pattern has shifted. Neither is addressed
by the other; the ability to reason about Lifecycle Policies is lost by not knowing usage, and the ability to
respond in real-time isn’t possible with Intelligent-Tiering because it only responds in retrospect once a
pattern has changed. Both mechanisms are therefore retrospective: they don’t predict how an object will
be accessed in the future or the number of objects that will be saved by a transition. This is exactly what
the research question in Section 1 requires to assess if an integrated framework can add, and under this
argument lies the motivation for the development of the case for predictive and forward-looking deci-
sion support throughout this review. AWS’ current tools are limited to simply following the rules, so the
next question is whether there’s an existing systems research approach to solving the forward-looking,
learned storage placement problem, and if there is, whether it can be adapted to an Amazon S3 context.
This is dealt with specifically in section 2.2.

2.2   Machine-Learning-Driven Storage Placement and Tiering
“Bring your own model” storage placement in WsC: cross-layer optimization for efficiency metrics is an
application-level approach presented by Yang et al. at MLSys 2025, which is strong on infrastructure-
scale placement, but is not optimized for consumer-facing storage services like Amazon S3 and is not
predictive of cost. Tu et al. (2025) also demonstrate the benefits of optimising the storage-hierarchy
throughput and tail latency with a mirror-optimised tiering strategy (MOST) in a storage-hierarchy sys-
tem, with the optimization of load rebalancing across tiers, but their evaluation is limited to an internal
caching-hierarchy instead of a public cloud storage-class model with true per-class pricing. Optimised
directly, Yang et al. make a placement decision based on optimisation of which application-level model
to use, whereas Tu et al., make a placement decision based on optimisation of load-balancing once a tier-
ing decision is made; both of these papers report a quality metric and neither explicitly report a dollar
figure on which a practitioner can act. Together, both works show that a learned placement is superior to
static placement rules or reactive placement rules – directly relevant to the research question’s premise
that an actionable and costed recommendation can be provided by pointing at a live Amazon S3 bucket –
but both do not recommend a placement algorithm that a cloud administrator could use for recommend-
ing an actionable and costed placement in a real Amazon S3 bucket. This is an immediate focus of this
proposal. Hence the learned placement is identified as being superior to static rules at the infrastructure
level but its translation to a costed per-object recommendation for a specific public-cloud service is left
open. A related but separate line of inquiry is: If a placement or tiering decision can be made, how to
optimize it for “cost” specifically, and has this been done for storage services like Amazon S3? The next
section (2.3) focuses on this literature.

2.3   Cost-Optimised and Workload-Aware Storage Architectures
Shen et al. (2025) propose TierBase, an IEEE International Conference on Data Engineering (ICDE
2025) (CORE: A*) published workload-driven, cost-optimised key-value store developed by Ant Group
which dynamically synchronises data between storage classes and cache. Liu et al. (2025) tackle a
complementary problem with SkyStore, published at Proceedings of the VLDB Endowment (VLDB
2025) (CORE: A* published independently from any single professional body, not IEEE), optimising
object storage cost across regions and cloud providers rather than across storage classes within a single
provider, which reported up to 6x cost savings over commercial multi-region baselines. Despite being a
peer reviewed, production-validated optimisation system that directly tests the premise of the research
question “workload-aware optimisation > static configuration”, both TierBase and SkyStore are limited
to the optimisation decision – rather than predicting future changes in the cost curve, nor estimating the
actual size of the resulting saving. This research brings the missing piece of top-tier systems papers –
that forward-looking forecasting component. TierBase and SkyStore, therefore, set it straight that it is
realistic and useful to optimise with cost awareness and workload considerations in production and this
is so only in hindsight or in retrospect and never in prediction. The validity of the forecasting layer that
this proposal proposes is a different question and will be discussed in the cloud FinOps and forecasting
literature next.

2.4   Predictive Cost Forecasting for Cloud FinOps
In Applied Intelligence (Springer, JCR-indexed), Beck, Dovern and Vogl (2025) examine how time-
series forecasting is used in macroeconomic and financial applications and demonstrate that a substan-
tial proportion of all forecasting research centers only on complex models, and not on any of these
models against a naive forecast, while only a handful of models perform better than a naive forecast on
series with low predictability. They have a different set of test domains, macroeconomics and finance,
but the methodological care taken is exactly as relevant to the research question addressed here as it
was in their domain of interest, and there is a forecasting component to any test of value only if it is
demonstrated to be superior to a naive persistence baseline, not just to seem correct in isolation. This
directly feeds into the evaluation design of the proposed framework (Section 3.3) which first tests the
ability of the forecasting model against a naive persistence baseline to give a credit for actual forecast-
ing ability, and not just headline accuracy. Bodra (2025) and Yakkanti (2025) separately make the case
for cloud financial governance to be dominated by the use of resource-allocation and cost-optimisation
algorithms based on machine learning, not as a methodology for forecasting, but rather as a practice.
Both papers are contrasted, with Bodra’s work being a comprehensive performance study of a myriad
of ML-based allocation algorithms over cloud resources more generally, and Yakkanti’s being closer
to the FinOps process but a bit more limited in scope; neither of these papers suggests or evaluates a
working pipeline that predicts allocation for storage-Class specifically, and thus their claims are aspira-
tional and not demonstrated in this context. While the forecasting literature provides a methodological
safety net (Beck, Dovern and Vogl, 2025), and FinOps provides a practice-level motivation (Bodra,
2025; Yakkanti, 2025), neither is applied, nor is their combination used to place and optimise the cost of
Amazon S3 storage classes. These four themes are integrated into the specific research niche in which
this proposal falls into, in Section 2.5.

2.5   Research Niche and Expected Contribution
In all the four themes above three recurring gaps are identified that relate directly to the research question
formulated in Section 1. AWS’s tooling (Section 2.1) is reactive, and provides no forward estimate on
cost savings, so it’s not designed to answer the question of whether or not a proactive alternative would
save cost. Second, the ML systems literature (Sections 2.2–2.3) shows, across four different top-tier,
peer-reviewed venues (MLSys, FAST, ICDE, VLDB), that learned, workload-aware placement and cost
optimisation outperforms static rules and reactive tooling, but the evidence is validated at the level of

storage hierarchies, key-value stores, or cross-cloud placement, and never as an Amazon S3-native, per-
object recommendation service that a practitioner could directly adopt. Third, the forecasting literature
and FinOps literature (Section 2.4) confirms the importance of the concept of predictive cost manage-
ment in principle, and provides some interesting methodological challenges because the literature is full
of claims of high predictive accuracy that one should be skeptical of—hiding the details of the prediction
process away from other teams, and not testing forecasting, storage-class recommendation and savings
estimation together in one pipeline, not to mention specifically testing the same concept on AWS S3.
This proposal’s niche is to fill the intersection of these three gaps: an AWS-native framework that recom-
mends a storage class, forecasting what that storage class will cost in the future, and estimating savings
of switching – but as Beck, Dovern and Vogl caution (2025), benchmarked against both AWS-native
tooling and a naive persistence baseline. The above works, each illustrate a single of these pieces of
the puzzle: learned placement, tiering, or cross-cloud cost optimisation, and the works of Bodra (2025)
and Yakkanti (2025) call for predictive FinOps, but without providing an empirically validated Amazon
S3 pipeline for recommendation, forecasting and savings estimation. To answer the research question,
therefore, requires original empirical work, a work that Section 3 aims to provide.

A paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test will be used to determine statistical significance of the cost out-
comes of the framework with each AWS-native baseline across repeated trial buckets (α = 0.05). The
Wilcoxon test is used when the number of trial buckets per workload type is expected to be small
(n ≈ 10–15 per workload), and since the cost differences between the two storage types are likely
not normally distributed (AWS storage-cost differences are typically right skewed), the Wilcoxon test is
an appropriate choice. Success is a statistically significant decrease in cost compared to both Lifecycle
Policies and Intelligent-Tiering across at least two of the three workload types, and a forecast error that is
statistically significantly better than the naive persistence baseline that was identified as a risk by Beck,
Dovern, and Vogl (2025).

3.5   Ethical Considerations
There are no personal or sensitive data involved in this research and no human participants. All test
objects held in the experimental S3 bucket will be synthetic dummy files which is to say the content of
the file is not analysed, only metadata (file size, storage class, file timestamps, file access counts). If the
public pricing or usage information is utilized to validate the forecasting module, then only AWS public
pricing history is used. The study is in the lowest-risk category in NCI’s ethics decision matrix (Public
data: Yes; Private data: No; Human subjects: No) and accompanied by a signed Declaration of Ethics
Consideration. AWS costs for experimentation will be constrained and monitored by budget alerts to
prevent cost creep and documented separately on the Cloud Resources Requirements Form.
