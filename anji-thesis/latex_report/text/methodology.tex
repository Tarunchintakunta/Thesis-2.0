\chapter{Research Methodology}

This chapter describes the experimental design, independent and dependent variables, hypotheses, statistical analysis plan, and validity considerations for investigating how Amazon SQS configuration affects message reliability and recovery under injected consumer and downstream failures.

\section{Research Design}

This study employs a \textbf{controlled experimental design} with systematic manipulation of configuration parameters and fault injection conditions. The research follows a quantitative, positivist paradigm: we measure observable metrics (message loss, duplicates, recovery time, throughput, latency) under controlled conditions to test pre-registered hypotheses and provide generalizable findings for practitioners.

\subsection{Experimental Approach}

The experiment is structured as a \textbf{two-arm comparison}:

\begin{description}
    \item[Control Arm (Synchronous API):] Orders are submitted directly to an HTTP API Lambda function that processes them synchronously, stores results in DynamoDB, and returns a response. This arm represents a traditional request-response architecture without queueing.
    
    \item[Treatment Arm (SQS Queue):] Orders are sent to an SQS Standard queue. A Lambda consumer function is triggered by the SQS event source mapping, processes batches of messages, stores results in DynamoDB, and deletes successfully processed messages. Failed messages are redelivered or moved to a dead-letter queue (DLQ) based on configuration.
\end{description}

The control arm serves two purposes: (1) it provides a baseline for comparing loss and latency under the same fault injection; (2) it validates that the fault injection mechanism itself does not artificially create failures unrelated to queue configuration.

\subsection{Baseline Replication}

Before introducing fault injection, the study replicates the steady-state experiments of \citet{Kyrychenko2025} to validate the artefact and simulator. This involves measuring throughput and latency across:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Visibility timeout: 30, 60, 120, 300, 600, 900 seconds
    \item Batch size: 1, 10, 25, 50 messages
    \item Delivery delay: 0, 5, 60, 300 seconds (subset)
    \item No faults injected
\end{itemize}

The goal is not exact numerical replication (the simulator abstracts AWS-specific timing), but confirmation that \emph{relative effects} match: batch size dominates throughput, visibility timeout does not affect steady-state throughput, duplicate rate is low (~0.002 from at-least-once delivery).

\section{Independent Variables}

The independent variables (IVs) manipulated in the experiment are:

\begin{description}
    \item[IV1: Visibility Timeout (VT)] The duration (in seconds) that a message remains invisible after being received by a consumer. Values: 30, 60, 120, 300, 600, 900. Rationale: Kyrychenko et al. identified VT as a key factor; this study tests how it affects recovery when consumers fail.
    
    \item[IV2: maxReceiveCount (MRC)] The number of times a message can be received before being moved to the DLQ. Values: 1, 3, 5, 10. Rationale: Controls the retry budget; low values quarantine messages quickly, high values tolerate longer outages but risk duplicate processing.
    
    \item[IV3: Batch Size] The number of messages delivered to the Lambda consumer in a single invocation. Values: 1, 10, 25, 50. Rationale: Affects throughput and the scope of partial failure reporting.
    
    \item[IV4: Fault Type] The type of failure injected. Values: \texttt{none} (baseline), \texttt{consumer\_kill} (consumer unavailable), \texttt{unhandled\_error} (exception thrown), \texttt{datastore\_reject} (DynamoDB write fails), \texttt{datastore\_timeout} (delayed write). Rationale: Different fault types may interact differently with VT and MRC.
    
    \item[IV5: Fault Timing] When the fault is active during the run. Configured as start and stop times (e.g., fault active 30–40 seconds into a 120-second run). Rationale: Ensures sufficient pre-fault and post-fault observation periods.
\end{description}

\section{Dependent Variables}

The dependent variables (DVs) measured as outcomes are:

\begin{description}
    \item[DV1: Message Loss] The percentage of orders sent that are neither successfully stored in DynamoDB nor captured in the DLQ. Formula: \( \text{loss} = 1 - (\text{stored} + \text{DLQ}) / \text{sent} \). Rationale: Primary reliability metric; zero loss is the goal.
    
    \item[DV2: Duplicate Rate] The number of duplicate stores per processed order. Formula: \( \text{duplicates} = (\text{stored} - \text{unique\_stored}) / \text{unique\_stored} \). Rationale: At-least-once delivery causes duplicates; configuration may amplify or reduce them under failure.
    
    \item[DV3: DLQ Capture Rate] The percentage of orders moved to the DLQ. Formula: \( \text{DLQ\_rate} = \text{DLQ} / \text{sent} \). Rationale: Indicates whether messages are being unnecessarily dead-lettered (too low MRC) or correctly quarantined (deterministic faults).
    
    \item[DV4: Recovery Time (Backlog)] The interval from failure cessation (fault stop time) until the queue backlog (visible + in-flight messages) returns to its pre-failure level. Measured in seconds. Rationale: Operational metric; shorter is better for responsiveness.
    
    \item[DV5: Recovery Time (Visible-Only)] Same as DV4 but counting only visible messages, excluding in-flight (not deleted yet). Rationale: Alternative definition; in-flight messages may be processing successfully during visibility timeout.
    
    \item[DV6: Throughput] Messages processed per second, measured as successful stores divided by total runtime. Rationale: Performance metric; should remain high during steady state and recover quickly after failure.
    
    \item[DV7: Latency] The time from message send to successful store, measured as the median across all orders. Rationale: User-perceived responsiveness; spikes during failure are expected, but should not persist after recovery.
\end{description}

\section{Hypotheses}

Three hypotheses are pre-registered to guide the analysis and prevent p-hacking:

\begin{description}
    \item[H1 (Message Loss):] Under injected consumer or downstream failures with DLQ configured, sufficient visibility timeout (≥ 900 seconds to allow full run horizon), and \texttt{maxReceiveCount} ≥ 3, message loss in the SQS queue arm is negligible (< 0.1\%) compared to the synchronous control arm (which may lose messages if the client retry budget is exhausted).
    
    \textbf{Rationale:} SQS redelivery and DLQ capture should prevent loss if the configuration allows enough retries and time for recovery. The hypothesis tests whether SQS reliability holds under failure.
    
    \textbf{Operationalization:} Compare mean loss rate for queue arm (configs with VT ≥ 900, MRC ≥ 3) vs. sync arm under each fault type. Use Mann-Whitney U test (non-parametric, as loss may not be normally distributed). Reject null (no difference) if p < 0.05 and effect size r > 0.3 (medium).
    
    \item[H2 (maxReceiveCount Independence):] After a \emph{transient} consumer failure (e.g., \texttt{consumer\_kill} for 10 seconds), recovery time does not differ significantly between \texttt{maxReceiveCount} = 1, 3, 5, and 10 when visibility timeout is held constant at 30 seconds.
    
    \textbf{Rationale:} Transient faults resolve before retry limits are reached (consumer recovers, messages redelivered and processed). Therefore, MRC should affect DLQ traffic (lower MRC may dead-letter some messages unnecessarily) but not recovery duration.
    
    \textbf{Operationalization:} Compare recovery time distributions for MRC ∈ \{1, 3, 5, 10\} under \texttt{consumer\_kill} with VT = 30. Use Kruskal-Wallis H test (non-parametric ANOVA for 4+ groups). Fail to reject null (no difference) if p ≥ 0.05 or effect size η² < 0.06 (small).
    
    \item[H3 (Steady-State Optimum Transfer):] The configuration that maximizes throughput in steady state (batch 50, visibility timeout 600, per \citet{Kyrychenko2025}) has \emph{significantly longer} recovery time than alternative configurations (e.g., batch 10, visibility timeout 30) under consumer failure.
    
    \textbf{Rationale:} Long visibility timeout delays redelivery; large batches may amplify backlog during failure. Configurations optimized for steady-state throughput may not minimize recovery time.
    
    \textbf{Operationalization:} Compare recovery time for (batch 50, VT 600) vs. pooled alternatives (batch 10, VT 30; batch 1, VT 30; batch 50, VT 30) under \texttt{consumer\_kill}. Use Mann-Whitney U test. Reject null (no difference) if p < 0.05 and effect size r > 0.5 (large).
\end{description}

\section{Statistical Analysis Plan}

To ensure rigorous, reproducible analysis, the following plan is pre-registered:

\subsection{Descriptive Statistics}

For each configuration cell (combination of VT, MRC, batch, fault), report:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Mean, median, standard deviation, min, max for each DV.
    \item 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals (1000 resamples) for means.
    \item Repeat count (number of trials) and total sample size.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hypothesis Testing}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Test Selection:} Use non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis H) as DVs like loss and recovery time are not guaranteed to be normally distributed and may have outliers.
    \item \textbf{Alpha Level:} Set α = 0.05 for each individual test.
    \item \textbf{Multiple Comparisons Correction:} Apply Holm-Bonferroni correction to control family-wise error rate across H1, H2, H3 (and any post-hoc pairwise tests). This is more powerful than Bonferroni while still controlling Type I error.
    \item \textbf{Effect Size:} Report rank-biserial r for two-group comparisons (H1, H3) and η² (epsilon-squared) for multi-group (H2). Interpret per Cohen's thresholds: r > 0.3 medium, > 0.5 large; η² > 0.06 medium, > 0.14 large.
    \item \textbf{Power Analysis:} Conduct post-hoc power analysis from pilot runs to ensure sufficient sample size for detecting medium-to-large effects (target power ≥ 0.80).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Visualizations}

Generate figures to communicate results:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Loss vs. visibility timeout (grouped by fault type)
    \item Recovery time vs. visibility timeout (grouped by fault type)
    \item Recovery time vs. maxReceiveCount (transient faults only)
    \item DLQ rate vs. maxReceiveCount (heatmap by fault type)
    \item Duplicates over baseline floor (bar chart by MRC)
    \item Timeline showing visible vs. total backlog during failure (line plot)
    \item Guidance transfer: scatter plot of throughput vs. recovery time for key configs
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Phases}

The experiment proceeds through seven phases:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pilot (18 runs):} Small-scale trial with 3 configs × 3 repeats × 2 arms to validate repeatability, tune simulator parameters, and estimate variance for power analysis.
    
    \item \textbf{Baseline Replication (115 runs):} No fault injection; replicate Kyrychenko et al. steady-state conditions (6 VT × 4 batch × ~5 repeats) to confirm simulator fidelity.
    
    \item \textbf{Arms Comparison (10 runs):} Sync vs. queue under no fault and under one fault type to validate that control arm behaves as expected.
    
    \item \textbf{Fault Campaigns A-E (~187 runs):} Systematic grid of VT × MRC × batch × fault type with sufficient repeats for hypothesis testing. Campaign A: consumer kill; B: unhandled error; C: datastore reject; D: datastore timeout; E: H3 grid (specific configs for steady-state optimum test).
    
    \item \textbf{Burst Replication (20 runs):} Replicate key cells under burst load (higher message rate) to check if findings generalize beyond steady load.
    
    \item \textbf{Statistical Analysis:} Run hypothesis tests, generate summary tables, compute effect sizes and CIs, apply Holm correction.
    
    \item \textbf{Figure Generation:} Produce all visualizations from aggregated results.
\end{enumerate}

Total: 350+ runs, all executed in the local simulator (DRY\_RUN=1). Each run is assigned a deterministic run ID (derived from config hash and seed) to enable reproducibility.

\section{Simulator Rationale}

All experimental results in this submission are generated from a discrete-event simulator (\texttt{src/localsim/engine.py}), not from live AWS infrastructure. This decision is justified by:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reproducibility:} Seeded random number generators and deterministic event ordering ensure that the same config produces the same results across runs. Live AWS introduces network variability, cold starts, and service events that are difficult to control.
    
    \item \textbf{Cost and Free-Tier Awareness:} Live AWS execution of the full matrix was not performed. \texttt{estimate\_cost.py} projects on the order of \$50--100+ for a comparable live campaign; that figure is an estimator output, not a measured Obj4 cost trade-off. The simulator runs in seconds with zero AWS charges. Reliability/recovery/latency were the measured DVs; monetary cost was not.
    
    \item \textbf{Ethical and Practical Feasibility:} The study uses the researcher's personal AWS account (no institutional or sponsored resources). Simulation avoids AWS quota limits and potential billing surprises.
    
    \item \textbf{Sufficient Fidelity for Relative Effects:} The simulator implements SQS Standard queue semantics (visibility timeout, receive count tracking, DLQ redrive, at-least-once delivery), Lambda event source mapping (batch processing, partial failure reporting via \texttt{ReportBatchItemFailures}), and DynamoDB idempotency checks. While absolute timing values differ from AWS (no cold starts, no network jitter), \emph{relative effects} (e.g., "recovery time increases linearly with visibility timeout") are expected to hold. Kyrychenko et al. provides the live AWS baseline for steady state; this work extends it to failure scenarios using the same configuration assumptions.
    
    \item \textbf{Validation Path:} The live backend code is written, unit-tested with moto, and included in the artefact (\texttt{src/control/live\_backend.py}). Future work can execute a subset of runs on AWS to quantify simulator fidelity (see \texttt{STATUS.md} and Conclusion for details).
\end{itemize}

The simulator's design and validation are described in detail in Chapter 4 (Design and Implementation).

\section{Threats to Validity}

\subsection{Internal Validity}

\textbf{Confounding variables:} The experiment controls VT, MRC, batch size, and fault type systematically. However, timing assumptions in the simulator (e.g., constant processing time per message, deterministic fault start/stop) may not reflect real-world variability. \emph{Mitigation:} Repeat trials (≥3 per config) and report variance; use non-parametric tests that do not assume normality.

\textbf{Instrumentation:} The simulator itself could contain bugs that affect results. \emph{Mitigation:} Unit tests (8 files, 40+ test cases), integration tests with moto, and manual validation of key scenarios (e.g., DLQ redrive, visibility timeout expiry).

\subsection{External Validity}

\textbf{Generalizability to live AWS:} Absolute metrics (e.g., exact recovery time in seconds) may differ from live AWS due to cold starts, network latency, and CloudWatch metric delays. \emph{Mitigation:} Focus interpretation on relative effects (e.g., "VT 600 takes 16× longer than VT 30") rather than absolute values. Live AWS validation in future work.

\textbf{Workload representativeness:} Synthetic order payloads (100–1000 bytes JSON) may not match all real-world SQS use cases. \emph{Mitigation:} The payload size and processing logic are representative of e-commerce, logging, and event-routing scenarios. Findings should generalize to similar workloads.

\textbf{Fault types:} Four fault types are modeled; real systems may experience others (AZ failures, SQS service events, gradual degradation). \emph{Mitigation:} The four faults span a representative space (transient vs. deterministic, consumer vs. downstream). Limitations are acknowledged in Conclusion.

\subsection{Construct Validity}

\textbf{Recovery time definition:} Multiple definitions exist (backlog return to baseline, visible-only, last message processed). The study reports both backlog and visible-only recovery time and discusses their implications. Practitioners should choose the definition that matches their monitoring tools and SLAs.

\textbf{Loss vs. DLQ:} A message moved to the DLQ is not "lost" (it is quarantined and auditable), but it is not successfully processed. The study separates these metrics and interprets loss as "neither stored nor DLQ'd."

\subsection{Conclusion Validity}

\textbf{Statistical power:} Pilot runs inform sample size; power analysis targets ≥0.80 for medium effects. Low-power tests risk Type II errors (failing to detect real effects). \emph{Mitigation:} Report power for each test; interpret non-significant results cautiously.

\textbf{Multiple comparisons:} Testing multiple hypotheses inflates Type I error (false positives). \emph{Mitigation:} Pre-register hypotheses; apply Holm-Bonferroni correction; report both raw and adjusted p-values.

\section{Ethical Considerations}

This research involves:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Synthetic data only:} No personally identifiable information (PII); order payloads are randomly generated JSON.
    \item \textbf{Researcher's own AWS account:} No institutional resources or third-party systems are affected. Simulation avoids any risk of unintended impact.
    \item \textbf{No human subjects:} No user studies, interviews, or surveys; purely technical experimentation.
\end{itemize}

The study aligns with NCI Ethics Scenario 2 (own-account AWS use) and Scenario 3 (synthetic data). No ethics review was required.

\section{Summary}

This chapter establishes the experimental design: a controlled, two-arm comparison with systematic manipulation of SQS configuration and fault injection conditions. The simulator enables reproducible, cost-free execution of 350+ runs. Three pre-registered hypotheses are tested using non-parametric statistics with Holm-Bonferroni correction. Threats to validity are acknowledged and mitigated where possible. The next chapter describes the artefact's design and implementation in detail.
